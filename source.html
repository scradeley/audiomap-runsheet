The wizard is essentially Audio device → System Topology → Gain structure → Identification & Positioning → Coverage area. Each page builds the picture of the system before we measure anything.

  • Topology is about defining the structure of the system at that point — how it's built and where the signal is handled:
    • some systems have a FOH console, some have an inline processor, some have the processing in the amps

Auto-capture captures all sources at all positions — everything in one pass, and it's spatially aware.

  • at each position it does simultaneous cross-correlation across all sources to understand its position
  • live mic tracking — real-time triangulation / trilateration continuously solves the mic's position as it moves through the spatial area, and draws it live on screen
  • then transfer-function capture with time-domain multiplexing across all sources
  • the result is an automated one-pass capture across the entire system — without manually choreographing your generator, routing, muting, alignment and capture, designing positioning and naming, then doing it over and over and trying to keep everything organised and from getting unwieldy
  • the spatially-aware auto-capture makes all of that smooth and easy

Once that's done we have all the data, and it creates all the right averages we need across the listening area — then you analyse it all in the time, frequency and spatial domains.

Say this

Traditional: align first → re-measure → then EQ. AudioMap: one capture, before & after computed together, feeding every EQ decision.

  • before vs after in time, frequency AND spatial — and it all carries through to the DSP / playback

The timing tab lets you specify how you want to time-align the system across multiple sources, with phase and coupling considerations.

  • different options depending on the job:
    • broadband time alignment — e.g. delay lines, or centre-fill / out-fill speakers
    • the more classic band-constrained multiway phase alignment — e.g. subwoofer to tops, or deeper multi-way crossovers
Say this — the workflow difference

The traditional approach usually means doing time alignment first, then taking all your measurements on an already-aligned system — which leaves you taking post-alignment measurements to build the transfer functions for EQ / frequency-domain correction. With AudioMap you capture everything in one pass, before-and-after time alignment is calculated from it, and that's taken into account when you consider any EQ adjustment.

Crossover-point alignment

A crossover-point alignment can be chosen specific to a frequency-domain crossover point — and you can pick any source, or even an average combination, as the anchor / timing reference.

  • optimise it to a specific listening position, or to averages across multiple positions for a larger coverage area
  • set the optimisation to minimise the worst-case errors, or to give the best average across all errors

And again — the results of any decision can be seen in the time, frequency and spatial domains, and they carry straight through to the audio engine / DSP and playback.

Tuning Tab

v2
  • auto-created, per-source — one tuning per box
    • stereo = separate L / R
New v2 · Groups

One aligned-sum tuning across a group. FOH: quick measures → parallel tuning on the master bus. Or per-section: tops / fills / subs.

Default — per-box (best result)

  • handles L-vs-R room differences
  • corrects at the source → max power per box, before the room varies it

How it's built

  • power average over coverage → magnitude curve to correct
  • inverse = target correction
  • algo adapts in real time to your DSP:
    • # bands IIR/FIR min/lin phase band-limited shelves parallel/serial
  • 4 bands → best fit · 8/12/24 → approaches flat
  • tilt targets over the flat correction — house curve, not just flat
  • every band visible — in-app or export

The tuning is created automatically, set up as a per-source individual multichannel tuning — one tuning per box. So with stereo, that's an individual tuning for the left and an individual tuning for the right.

New in v2 · Groups

You can now have an aligned-sum tuning across a group of sources. That's more of a FOH workflow — come into a venue, take a few quick measurements, and insert a parallel tuning over the master bus for the whole system. Or apply it to parts of the system — tops, centre fills, subs — without going down to each individual speaker.

Default — per-box, and it's the best result

The default is that each individual speaker is corrected on its own. So if there are differences between the left and right side of the room, it takes that into account and really dials things in. You get maximum power handling from each box, and a lot of what's corrected happens at the source — not once it's gone through the room, which varies across every position. So individual tuning will always give you a better result.

How each tuning is built

The tunings are created automatically. It starts with the power average across the coverage area of the measurements — a frequency-domain magnitude curve we're aiming to correct. We take the inverse of that curve as the target correction.

Of course it depends on your DSP and how much processing you have — so AudioMap adjusts intelligently, with a sophisticated algorithm, in real time as you change parameters:

  • how many EQ bands your processor has
  • IIR or FIR processing
  • minimum phase or linear phase
  • whether to only correct within certain bands
  • whether the processing has shelving EQs
  • whether the setup allows parallel processing (a wet/dry path) or is pure serial

Plainly: with a 4-band EQ you can watch the algorithm minimise the area under the curve for the best correction you can get. Dial up to 8, 12 or 24 bands and the corrected response gets flatter and flatter.

  • Tilt targets sit over the top of the flat correction — so you can aim for a house / voicing curve rather than dead flat
  • you can see every EQ band applied — all of it available in-app or via export

Playback + Preview

  • listen to your decisions in real time
  • full built-in DSP + mixer stage
  • two engines:
    • internal — test signals, measure, capture
    • external (extracted, host/client) — playback
    • seamless switch via Playback mode
  • virtual sound card:
    • grabs system audio (Spotify, YouTube…) → correction → out
    • or feed external audio in
  • external engine standalone:
    • close main app → light playback UI keeps running
    • run DSP as a service on server / hardware; control from laptop or phone

Then you can preview and actually listen back to the results of the decisions you make — in real time. AudioMap has a full built-in DSP processing and mix stage, which you can see through the mixer.

  • there are two audio engines:
    • internal — used for generating test signals, measurement and capture
    • external — extracted out of the main app, working host/client, used for playback
    • you switch between the two seamlessly by activating Playback mode
  • this let us build in a virtual sound card to capture the system audio:
    • play from your application layer — Spotify, YouTube, etc. — straight into AudioMap correction and out through your system
    • or run external audio in, through the processing, and out to your system
  • because the external engine is extracted, it can run as its own system process:
    • close the main app and keep a small system process running with a lighter UI — just for playback, without all the measurement stuff
    • or run the DSP as its own service on a server or external hardware, and remotely control / monitor it from a laptop or mobile

Correct where it's effective — don't try to correct where the physics won't allow it.

That's the basic workflow — there are some more advanced features too.

  • Heatmaps — the measured field across the space
  • Vector-based analysis in the time domain:
    • see the directional information of your impulse response and how it fits within a spatial model
    • useful for understanding your room and its reflections
  • Room inference — detects the boundaries of your room based on those first- and second-order reflections
  • Room-mode analysis — derived from the actual measurement data
  • Minimum & excess phase:
    • understand the phase shift introduced at the source / speaker level, versus the phase shift from reflections, room modes, or a highly reverberant space
Say this

This lets you correct where correction will be effective — and not attempt to correct where the physics simply don't allow for it.
